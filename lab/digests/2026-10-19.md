## Physics concept of the day: why bigger orbits have bigger time steps

Last night produced no results to hang this on. So here is the idea behind the lab's most-used quantity, `accel_step`.

**The ML bridge.** Suppose you resample every training sequence to a fixed length, whatever its real duration. A long sequence then gets a coarser time resolution per token than a short one. The model sees "token to token", not "second to second".

**The physics.** The lab does exactly this with orbits: every orbit is cut into $N$ snapshots. Kepler's third law says the period grows with orbit size: $P^2 \propto a^3$, where $P$ is the period and $a$ is the semi-major axis (roughly the orbit's average radius). So the time between snapshots is $\Delta t = P/N$, which is longer for big orbits. Squaring it gives $\Delta t^2 \propto a^3$.

To predict the next position, the model needs the change in velocity per snapshot. That is the physical acceleration $1/r^2$ (with $G=1$, primary mass 1, and $r$ the current separation) multiplied by $\Delta t^2$. Up to a constant, this gives the $a^3/r^2$ form of `accel_step`.

**The tie-back.** There is no number from last night to anchor this. The thing to keep in mind is that a probe can fit `accel_step` well without the model having learned "force" as a physicist would. The real test is whether the $1/r^2$ dependence on separation shows up within a single orbit. Many of the lab's 52 replicated findings rest on this distinction. Vafa et al. (2025) is the closest outside work: their transformer predicted orbits well but implied a force law that was not Newton's.

**Quick check:** is $\Delta t$ for an orbit with twice the semi-major axis twice as long, or more? (Hint: $\Delta t \propto a^{3/2}$.)
