## Physics concept of the day: why bigger orbits take bigger steps

With no new results, this is a good morning to firm up the idea behind a lot of the lab's past findings: **the model never sees time, only snapshots**.

**The ML bridge.** Imagine training a sequence model on videos where every clip has the same number of frames per loop, but some loops are slow and some are fast. The model can't know the true frame rate. It only learns "how much things change per frame". Any physical law it picks up will be quietly rescaled by that unknown frame interval.

**The physics.** Each simulated orbit is sampled at a fixed $N$ snapshots per orbit, so the time between snapshots is $\Delta t = P/N$, where $P$ is the orbital period. Kepler's third law says $P^2 \propto a^3$, where $a$ is the semi-major axis, i.e. the orbit's size. So bigger orbits have longer periods, and therefore longer gaps between snapshots.

What the model needs for next-step prediction is the change in velocity per snapshot. That is the physical acceleration ($\propto 1/r^2$, where $r$ is the current separation) multiplied by $\Delta t^2$:

$$a_{\text{step}} \propto \frac{a^3}{r^2}$$

This is the lab's `accel_step` target. Two consequences:
1. A model that "encodes `accel_step` better than force" has learned the gravity its task actually needs, measured in snapshot units.
2. The real test of having learned Newton is whether, *within one orbit*, the representation tracks $1/r^2$. Across orbits, the $a^3$ factor dominates and can disguise what was learned.

This is also why symbolic fits that come out as $a^2/r$ instead of $a^3/r^2$ are interesting. That pattern echoes **Vafa et al. (2025)**, where a transformer predicted orbits well but implied a non-Newtonian force law.
