\section{Abstract}

Do small transformers trained to predict simulated two-body orbits ($G = M = 1$) learn the gravitational law, or shortcuts that happen to work, and when does any structure form? We fit linear probes to the residual stream for physical force $|F| = m_2/r^2$, per-snapshot acceleration \texttt{accel\_step} $= (2\pi/N)^2 a^3/((1+m_2)r^2)$, distance and energy, and judge each against several randomly initialised controls. Across 39 replicated findings (27 supported, 12 refuted; 95 evidence runs, 4 cut short by the budget stop), \texttt{accel\_step} beats its control in every setting tested. At $N = 64$ it forms at steps 85–140 in six seeds, before the model beats constant-velocity extrapolation [\texttt{f-20261012-002713-8d93}, \texttt{f-20261012-005950-42a7}]. Its headroom-normalised margin stays at 0.63–0.75 from $N = 16$ to $N = 256$ [\texttt{f-20261013-000329-a86b}, \texttt{f-20261013-075855-f75f}, \texttt{f-20261013-051807-f3f1}, \texttt{f-20261013-063631-c4ed}]. Force decodability falls with sampling density instead: margins are 0.216–0.255, 0.079–0.143 and 0.026–0.045 at $N = 64$, 128 and 256 [\texttt{f-20261013-075855-f75f}, \texttt{f-20261013-075824-35f0}, \texttt{f-20261013-075749-3d2c}]. The main negative result is that symbolic fits to the probe directions give $r^{-1}$, not $r^{-2}$, in all four seeds tested. The fitter itself has not yet been checked on the true target [\texttt{f-20261011-231118-7a37}, \texttt{f-20261011-223027-8fb3}].

\section{Methods}

\subsection{Data generation}

All orbits are simulated with REBOUND in units where $G = 1$ and the primary mass is $M = 1$. The gravitational force on body 2 is therefore $|F| = m_2/r^2$, where $m_2$ is the mass ratio. Varying $M$ would add nothing. Positions are sampled at fixed phase intervals, so absolute mass and time scale cannot be identified from them.

Each orbit is sampled at a fixed number of snapshots per orbit, $N$ (\texttt{steps\_per\_orbit}). The time between snapshots is therefore $\Delta t = P/N$, which grows with orbit size ($\Delta t^2 \propto a^3$). The model never sees physical time.

Three-body systems are hierarchical: the third body sits at 3.5–6× the inner semi-major axis. They are used only as an evaluation set for models trained on two-body systems.

The configuration schema adds these fields to the specification:

\begin{itemize}
\item \texttt{data.eccentricity\_range}
\item \texttt{data.data\_seed}
\item \texttt{probes.control\_margin}
\item \texttt{probes.patching}
\item \texttt{probes.symbolic}
\item \texttt{eval\_sets}, for out-of-distribution evaluation
\end{itemize}

Unknown fields are rejected. The training ranges differ between the cited experiments:

\begin{itemize}
\item Some runs train on the default range, which reaches $e \leq 0.5$ [\texttt{f-20261011-223027-8fb3}].
\item Some runs widen the mass ratio to $m_2 \in [0.01, 1]$ [\texttt{f-20261011-203628-5cd9}].
\item Some runs widen eccentricity to $e \in [0, 0.8]$ [\texttt{f-20261011-211551-d718}].
\item Some runs widen both, to $e \in [0, 0.9]$ and $m_2 \in [0.01, 1]$ [\texttt{f-20261011-223027-8fb3}]. Both of these runs share data seed 0.
\end{itemize}

The held-out evaluation sets are:

\begin{itemize}
\item low mass ratio, $m_2 \in [0.01, 0.1]$
\item high eccentricity, $e \in [0.5, 0.8]$
\item three-body systems [\texttt{f-20261011-203723-b7de}]
\item held-out low-$e$ and high-$e$ splits, used for the wide-eccentricity runs [\texttt{f-20261011-211551-d718}]
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Tokens, model and prediction target}

Each token is one time step. It holds the $xy$ positions of up to three bodies (6 numbers) in the centre-of-mass frame, with absent bodies set to zero. Because every system uses the same token format, a model trained on two bodies can be evaluated on three-body sets.

The model predicts the normalised displacement to the next step. All evidence runs use one transformer architecture: $L = 4$ layers, width $d = 64$, and $N = 64$ snapshots per orbit [\texttt{f-20261011-211551-d718}].

Prediction quality is reported as \texttt{val\_mse\_ratio}. This is the model's MSE divided by the MSE of constant-velocity extrapolation, $\hat{x}_{t+1} = 2x_t - x_{t-1}$. Both are computed in physical position units over steps $t \geq 1$ on the same held-out systems. A value below 1 means the model beats the baseline.

\subsection{Training}

The lab's default first-experiment (E1) configuration trains for 5k steps at batch size 128 and learning rate $10^{-3}$. Training runs on a laptop GPU (MPS) and is kept CPU-friendly.

Checkpoint spacing varies between experiments. One formation experiment probes every 250 steps [\texttt{f-20261011-201533-0653}]. A dense-checkpoint experiment probes every 25 steps early in training [\texttt{f-20261011-203659-4e2f}].

Every finding is replicated on two training seeds.

\textbf{Budget hard stop.} A runaway guard stops a run once it reaches 2× its seed's share of \texttt{budget.max\_compute\_hours}. The clock starts at the first training step, so dataset generation does not count. A stopped run is checkpointed and probed at its stop step. It records \texttt{steps\_completed} and \texttt{steps\_planned}, and its \texttt{completion} is the fraction of planned steps it trained. Runs below 80\% completion do not count toward replication.

Two of the 12 evidence runs were cut short by an earlier version of this guard. That version stopped at 1.2× the budget share and counted dataset generation against it. Both runs are in [\texttt{f-20261011-211551-d718}]: seed 9 stopped at completion 0.867 (step 2640 of 3000) and seed 8 at 0.9 (step 2714). Under that version the probes were taken at the last regular checkpoint, step 2500, not at the stop step. Their \texttt{val\_mse\_ratio} was still falling when training stopped.

\subsection{Probe targets}

Linear probes read five quantities from the residual stream at every layer:

\begin{itemize}
\item \texttt{distance}
\item relative \texttt{velocity}
\item \texttt{energy}, which is constant per system
\item physical \texttt{force}, $|F| = m_2/r^2$
\item the per-snapshot acceleration \texttt{accel\_step}:
\end{itemize}

\[|a_{\text{step}}| = \frac{F}{m_2}\,\Delta t^2 = \left(\frac{2\pi}{N}\right)^2 \frac{a^3}{(1+m_2)\,r^2}.\]

Next-step prediction needs \texttt{accel\_step}, not the physical force. A weak \texttt{force} probe on its own could therefore mean the model learned a per-snapshot version of gravity, not that it learned no gravity. For this reason \texttt{force} and \texttt{accel\_step} are always compared.

A unit test checks two properties of \texttt{accel\_step}:

\begin{itemize}
\item It equals the second difference of positions, to within 1\%.
\item It is not proportional to the physical force across orbit sizes.
\end{itemize}

\texttt{velocity} probe results do not appear in any cited finding.

\subsection{Probe protocol and random-model controls}

Probes run on 300 held-out systems, at every other time step from $t = 8$. Systems are split 70/30 into probe-train and probe-test sets, so rows from the same orbit never appear on both sides.

Each probe is a ridge regression (RidgeCV over $\alpha \in [10^{-3}, 10^{4}]$) on standardised inputs and outputs. $R^2$ is averaged across output dimensions.

Every probe has a control: the same probe fitted on a randomly initialised model of the same architecture. There is one control per layer, because a random model does not change across checkpoints. A probe result counts only if

\[R^2 - R^2_{\text{control}} \geq 0.1 \quad (\texttt{probes.control\_margin}).\]

For each target, the lab reports:

\begin{itemize}
\item the best layer
\item its $R^2$ and control $R^2$
\item \texttt{formed\_at\_step}, the first checkpoint at which the margin reaches the threshold (null if it never does)
\end{itemize}

The control $R^2$ for \texttt{accel\_step} is high under the default ranges (0.71–0.75). This caps the margin \texttt{accel\_step} can reach. One finding therefore also reports a post hoc, headroom-normalised score, $(R^2 - R^2_{\text{control}})/(1 - R^2_{\text{control}})$, but its pre-registered criterion uses the raw margin [\texttt{f-20261011-203628-5cd9}].

Probes on the out-of-distribution evaluation sets have no random controls. Their $R^2$ values therefore cannot be read as margins [\texttt{f-20261011-203723-b7de}, \texttt{f-20261011-211551-d718}].

Only the final checkpoint's activations are saved to disk, as float16. Activations for earlier checkpoints are recomputed from the saved weights when needed.

\subsection{Symbolic regression}

When \texttt{probes.symbolic} is enabled, PySR fits a closed-form expression to the best-layer probe direction for \texttt{force} and for \texttt{accel\_step}. The \texttt{accel\_step} fit uses the inputs $r$, $m_2$ and $a$. Each fit reports:

\begin{itemize}
\item the equation
\item whether the true law was recovered (\texttt{law\_recovered})
\item \texttt{fit\_error\_vs\_true\_law}
\end{itemize}

Symbolic fits were run at the final checkpoint only, and only in the runs behind [\texttt{f-20261011-223027-8fb3}]. These are the only evidence runs with fitted equations.

\subsection{Activation patching}

The pipeline also supports activation patching. Heads are ranked by a force probe on each head's contribution to the residual stream. The top three heads, plus the lowest-ranked head as a control, are each patched with that head's output on a different system, and the relative increase in next-step MSE is recorded. None of the cited findings report patching results.

\section{Results}

The lab's records contain 39 citable findings, all with status \texttt{replicated}. Of these, 27 support their pre-registered hypothesis and 12 refute it. Together they draw on 95 evidence runs. The budget hard stop cut 4 of those runs short, and we flag those runs wherever their numbers appear. Unless we say otherwise, the model has 4 layers and $d_{\text{model}} = 64$, is trained on data seed 0, and is probed with linear ridge probes. A probe "beats control" only if $R^2 - \text{control } R^2 \ge \max(0.1, \text{control spread})$. Older findings from 2026-10-11 report a single random control. Their \texttt{distance} and \texttt{energy} margins should therefore be read with caution, because random models differ by up to about 0.2 on those targets.

\subsection{Prediction quality}

Every model trained to completion at $N \le 128$ beats constant-velocity extrapolation:

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Default setting.} Seeds 2 and 3 reached a final \texttt{val\_mse\_ratio} of about 0.011 [\texttt{f-20261011-201533-0653}].
\item \textbf{$N = 16$.} The ratio was 0.0007 [\texttt{f-20261013-000329-a86b}].
\item \textbf{6 layers, $d_{\text{model}} = 128$, $e \le 0.85$.} The ratio was 0.0032–0.0037 [\texttt{f-20261012-030233-ab28}].
\end{itemize}

The exception is $N = 256$ at $e \le 0.85$. After 600 steps these models never beat the baseline, ending at 2.03–2.37 [\texttt{f-20261012-034134-5e82}]. At $e \le 0.5$, $N = 256$ models cross below 1 only around step 2000 and end at 0.24–0.32 after 3000 steps [\texttt{f-20261013-063603-95b5}, \texttt{f-20261013-075749-3d2c}].

\subsection{When structure forms}

\textbf{Geometry forms first, then \texttt{accel\_step}, then (sometimes) force.} In every timing experiment, \texttt{distance} and \texttt{energy} beat their controls within the first few checkpoints, at steps 5–50. At that point \texttt{val\_mse\_ratio} is still far above 1 [\texttt{f-20261011-203659-4e2f}, \texttt{f-20261012-002713-8d93}, \texttt{f-20261012-005950-42a7}, \texttt{f-20261012-044142-d490}]. \texttt{accel\_step} follows at layer 4. Its formation step is stable across seeds and checkpoint spacings:

| Setting | \texttt{accel\_step} formation step | \texttt{val\_mse\_ratio} < 1 first at |
|---|---|---|
| 25-step checkpoints, seeds 4–5 [\texttt{f-20261011-203659-4e2f}] | 100, 100 | 175 |
| 10-step, seeds 44–46 [\texttt{f-20261012-002713-8d93}] | 140, 140, 100 | 180, 180, 190 |
| 10-step, best-of-5 controls, seeds 47–49 [\texttt{f-20261012-005950-42a7}] | 85, 85, 90 | 135–165 |
| 10-step, seeds 62–64 [\texttt{f-20261012-044142-d490}] | 90, 100, 100 | 140–160 |
| 5-step, seeds 65–69 [\texttt{f-20261012-054929-0067}] | 65–90 | — |

At $N = 64$ and $e \le 0.5$, then, the per-snapshot acceleration code becomes decodable 40–90 steps before the model beats the baseline. The margins at the crossing are thin, though: 0.100–0.115 against a bar of 0.1 [\texttt{f-20261011-203659-4e2f}, \texttt{f-20261012-002713-8d93}].

!\href{figure:formation/run-20261011-234250-a7ca}{Probe formation over training, N = 64, seed 46, 10-step checkpoints}

\textbf{The "before the baseline" ordering is not a stable marker.} Three experiments changed one factor and moved the formation step very little:

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Sampling density.} At $N = 16$, \texttt{val\_mse\_ratio} falls below 1 at step 20, but \texttt{accel\_step} still forms at steps 75–90. That is within 5–15 steps of the seed-matched $N = 64$ runs. The ordering flips because the baseline is weaker at $N = 16$, not because formation moves [\texttt{f-20261012-015955-fc2b}].
\item \textbf{$N = 256$.} At $e \le 0.85$, \texttt{accel\_step} forms at steps 60–70, the same steps as at $N = 64$, even though these models never beat the baseline [\texttt{f-20261012-034134-5e82}, \texttt{f-20261012-034018-5526}].
\item \textbf{Learning rate.} Halving lr delayed formation by only 10–30 steps (to steps 70/80/100), not the roughly 2× that proportional scaling predicts [\texttt{f-20261012-044258-ab88}].
\end{itemize}

Formation therefore tracks a window of training steps more closely than it tracks loss relative to the baseline. A narrow pre-registered window for $N = 64$ formation was refuted: only 2 of 5 seeds fell inside it, and the misses were 5–10 steps early [\texttt{f-20261012-054929-0067}].

\textbf{An early force transient.} Every seed with 5–10-step checkpoints shows the same brief force signal: a margin of about 0.11–0.19 at steps 10–25 that falls to about 0 by steps 40–50. The transient survives best-of-5 controls [\texttt{f-20261012-005950-42a7}, \texttt{f-20261012-044142-d490}, \texttt{f-20261012-015955-fc2b}, \texttt{f-20261012-054929-0067}]. We don't know what it reflects.

\textbf{In short runs force never forms; in longer runs it forms late.}

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Short runs (300 steps).} Force fails its control in most seeds. Final margins are 0.000–0.080 [\texttt{f-20261012-002713-8d93}, \texttt{f-20261012-044142-d490}], with borderline exceptions of 0.103 and 0.105 [\texttt{f-20261012-054929-0067}].
\item \textbf{Longer runs.} Force crosses the bar at steps 350–600, against step 100–150 for \texttt{accel\_step} [\texttt{f-20261013-020028-8792}, \texttt{f-20261013-025812-b9ee}].
\item \textbf{The short-run null is partly a stopping artefact.} In 1500-step runs the step-600 force margin is already 0.104–0.138, while 600-step runs with the same seeds were at 0.085–0.099 [\texttt{f-20261013-025812-b9ee}].
\item \textbf{Force keeps rising.} From step 500 to 5000 force gains 0.074–0.081, while \texttt{accel\_step} gains only 0.022–0.025 [\texttt{f-20261013-020028-8792}].
\end{itemize}

\subsection{\texttt{accel\_step} versus force}

\textbf{Which code "leads" depends on the metric.} On raw margin, force often wins:

\begin{itemize}
\item 0.243/0.235 vs 0.205/0.175 with $m_2 \in [0.01, 1]$ [\texttt{f-20261011-203628-5cd9}];
\item 0.221/0.255 vs 0.146/0.128 in a 2-layer, $d_{\text{model}} = 32$ model [\texttt{f-20261013-000220-ab2d}];
\item 0.236/0.275 vs 0.131/0.140 at $N = 16$ [\texttt{f-20261013-000329-a86b}].
\end{itemize}

This happens because the \texttt{accel\_step} control is high, around 0.65–0.81 at $e \le 0.5$. Normalised by headroom, $(R^2 - c)/(1 - c)$, \texttt{accel\_step} leads in every multi-seed comparison except the 2-layer model, where each code wins in one seed [\texttt{f-20261013-000220-ab2d}]:

| Setting | \texttt{accel\_step} (normalised) | force (normalised) |
|---|---|---|
| Wide $m_2$ range [\texttt{f-20261011-203628-5cd9}] | 0.70, 0.71 | 0.48, 0.42 |
| 6 layers, $d_{\text{model}} = 128$, $e \le 0.85$ [\texttt{f-20261012-030233-ab28}] | 0.66, 0.61 | 0.41, 0.41 |
| $N = 64$, seeds 22/23 [\texttt{f-20261013-015914-3f49}] | 0.68, 0.70 | 0.45, 0.43 |
| $N = 64$, seeds 24–26 [\texttt{f-20261013-075855-f75f}] | 0.71, 0.69, 0.68 | ≈0.43–0.48 |
| $N = 64$, data seed 1 [\texttt{f-20261013-054512-1b08}] | 0.725, 0.740 | 0.44, 0.45 |
| $N = 64$, data seed 1, 1500 steps [\texttt{f-20261013-025812-b9ee}] | 0.66–0.71 | 0.35–0.43 |
| $d_{\text{model}} = 128$, data seed 1 [\texttt{f-20261013-020028-8792}] | 0.86, 0.83 | 0.49, 0.53 |

!\href{figure:heatmap/run-20261013-074422-d66a/accel_step}{accel\_step probe heatmap, N = 64, seed 24}

\textbf{Confound: force may be a read of distance.} At $N \le 128$, force usually peaks at layer 1, where \texttt{distance} is also decoded best [\texttt{f-20261013-015914-3f49}, \texttt{f-20261013-051738-2a37}, \texttt{f-20261013-075855-f75f}, \texttt{f-20261013-224908-8b46}]. In the 1500-step data-seed-1 runs, layer-4 force never exceeded about 0.085 [\texttt{f-20261013-025812-b9ee}]. No probe with \texttt{distance} regressed out has been run. We therefore treat force margins as force \emph{decodability}, not as evidence for a separate $m_2$-weighted representation.

\textbf{Fixing the mass ratio did not lift force.} With $m_2 = 0.5$, the force target is exactly $0.5/r^2$. Even so, force $R^2$ stayed at 0.750 and 0.732, while \texttt{distance} decoded at 0.87–0.89 [\texttt{f-20261011-223000-b5f9}]. Both runs were cut short by the budget stop. Seed 14 completed 0.55 of its steps, which is below the 80\% replication threshold, and seed 15 completed 0.917. The result therefore rests mainly on seed 15.

\textbf{Width and depth.} In the 6-layer, $d_{\text{model}} = 128$ model at $e \le 0.85$, linear \texttt{accel\_step} $R^2$ levelled off at 0.70–0.74. Over the same steps \texttt{val\_mse\_ratio} fell a further 5.5–6.8×. Linear decodability thus stops tracking prediction quality [\texttt{f-20261012-030233-ab28}].

\subsection{Sampling density: force decodability falls with $N$, \texttt{accel\_step} does not}

Final force margins after 3000 steps:

| | $N = 16$ | $N = 64$ | $N = 128$ | $N = 256$ |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Data seed 0, seeds 24/25/26 | — | 0.228 / 0.255 / 0.216 [\texttt{f-20261013-075855-f75f}] | 0.128 / 0.143 / 0.079 [\texttt{f-20261013-075824-35f0}] | 0.026 / 0.045 / 0.029 [\texttt{f-20261013-075749-3d2c}] |
| Data seed 0, seeds 22/23 | — | 0.247 / 0.234 [\texttt{f-20261013-015914-3f49}] | 0.108 / 0.094 [\texttt{f-20261013-051807-f3f1}] | 0.040 / 0.033 [\texttt{f-20261013-063603-95b5}] |
| Data seed 0, seeds 20/21 | 0.236 / 0.275 [\texttt{f-20261013-000329-a86b}] | — | — | 0.113 / 0.003 (rerun) [\texttt{f-20261013-050513-2c7a}] |
| Data seed 1, seeds 20/21 | — | 0.209 / 0.209 [\texttt{f-20261013-054512-1b08}] | 0.137 / 0.145 [\texttt{f-20261013-054542-23e1}] | 0.072 / 0.039 [\texttt{f-20261013-063631-c4ed}] |
| Data seed 1, seeds 22/23 | — | ≈0.268 / ≈0.275 | 0.173 / 0.163 [\texttt{f-20261013-224908-8b46}] | — |

The $N = 64$ values for data seed 1, seeds 22/23, come from the paired arm's rounded trajectory as reported in [\texttt{f-20261013-224908-8b46}].

\textbf{$N = 16$ and $N = 64$ give the same force level}, so a gradual decline across all $N$ was refuted [\texttt{f-20261013-000329-a86b}, \texttt{f-20261013-015914-3f49}].

\textbf{From $N = 64$ to $N = 128$, the trained model loses force decodability.} The controls barely move:

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Data seed 0, seeds 24–26.} Trained force $R^2$ fell by 0.098–0.128. Controls moved by +0.002 to +0.009, and \texttt{distance} $R^2$ did not fall [\texttt{f-20261013-075824-35f0}].
\item \textbf{Data seed 0, seeds 22/23.} The same pattern holds [\texttt{f-20261013-051807-f3f1}].
\item \textbf{Data seed 1, seeds 20/21.} The drop was smaller, 0.064–0.072 [\texttt{f-20261013-054542-23e1}].
\item \textbf{Data seed 1, seeds 22/23 (new).} Trained force $R^2$ fell by 0.095–0.097, controls changed by −0.002 to +0.017, and layer-1 \texttt{distance} $R^2$ held or rose (0.933/0.935 vs 0.896/0.932). The loss is therefore specific to force [\texttt{f-20261013-224908-8b46}]. Within data seed 1, the spread between model seeds (0.064–0.072 vs 0.095–0.112 in margin) is as large as the difference between data seeds 0 and 1.
\item \textbf{Long training does not close the gap.} After 12000 steps, $N = 64$ still sat 0.062 and 0.069 above $N = 128$ [\texttt{f-20261013-051738-2a37}].
\end{itemize}

\textbf{From $N = 128$ to $N = 256$, the cause of the drop depends on the dataset.}

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Data seed 1.} Trained force $R^2$ did not fall (about 0.66 → 0.671–0.672). Controls rose from 0.52 to 0.60–0.63, so the whole drop is a control effect [\texttt{f-20261013-063631-c4ed}].
\item \textbf{Data seed 0, seeds 24–26.} Trained $R^2$ also fell, by 0.023–0.076, with controls accounting for roughly 35–50\% of the margin loss [\texttt{f-20261013-075749-3d2c}].
\item \textbf{Data seed 0, seeds 22/23.} The split is intermediate [\texttt{f-20261013-063603-95b5}].
\end{itemize}

!\href{figure:heatmap/run-20261013-061904-9009/force}{Force probe heatmap, N = 256, seed 22}

\textbf{On data seed 0, the $N = 256$ shortfall is not just slower convergence.}

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{1000 more steps.} Training to 4000 steps raised trained force $R^2$ by only about 0.011–0.014. The gap to $N = 128$ stayed at 0.053–0.082, and force still failed its control [\texttt{f-20261013-083108-2efd}].
\item \textbf{Matched on \texttt{val\_mse\_ratio}.} Compared at a matched ratio, $N = 128$ still decoded force better: 0.641 vs 0.557 and 0.630 vs 0.569 [\texttt{f-20261013-093210-2e03}].
\end{itemize}

This rests on two seeds on one dataset, and force was still gaining 0.005–0.007 margin per 500 steps. A fixed ceiling has not been shown [\texttt{f-20261013-083108-2efd}].

\textbf{The normalised \texttt{accel\_step} margin is flat across $N$:}

\begin{itemize}
\item 0.68–0.69 at $N = 16$ [\texttt{f-20261013-000329-a86b}];
\item 0.68–0.74 at $N = 64$ [\texttt{f-20261013-015914-3f49}, \texttt{f-20261013-075855-f75f}, \texttt{f-20261013-054512-1b08}];
\item 0.63–0.72 at $N = 128$ [\texttt{f-20261013-051807-f3f1}, \texttt{f-20261013-075824-35f0}, \texttt{f-20261013-054542-23e1}, \texttt{f-20261013-093210-2e03}, \texttt{f-20261013-224908-8b46}];
\item 0.66–0.75 at $N = 256$ [\texttt{f-20261013-063603-95b5}, \texttt{f-20261013-063631-c4ed}, \texttt{f-20261013-075749-3d2c}, \texttt{f-20261013-083108-2efd}].
\end{itemize}

The per-snapshot acceleration code is equally readable at every sampling density we tested. Linear force decodability falls with $N$ above 64 in every paired seed.

\subsection{Reproducibility}

The original $N = 256$ reference runs did not reproduce. An exact rerun gave force margins of 0.113 and 0.003, against the original 0.033–0.040, and \texttt{accel\_step} margins 0.033–0.083 away from the originals. At step 3000 the rerun agreed with the 6000- and 12000-step runs, which rules out the learning-rate schedule horizon as the cause [\texttt{f-20261013-050513-2c7a}]. A second identical rerun matched the first to 3 decimals at every checkpoint [\texttt{f-20261013-054603-d6cc}]. So under the current pipeline, training and probing are deterministic for a fixed seed. The 0.11 force gap between seeds 20 and 21 is genuine seed variance, and the original runs remain unexplained.

\subsection{Eccentricity sets the control level}

Widening the training range of $e$ lowers both the \texttt{accel\_step} control and the trained $R^2$ (all $N = 64$):

| $e$ range | control $R^2$ | trained $R^2$ | normalised margin |
|---|---|---|---|
| $\le 0.5$ [\texttt{f-20261012-044142-d490}] | 0.724–0.746 | 0.890–0.902 | — |
| $\le 0.7$ [\texttt{f-20261012-055051-b178}] | 0.543–0.559 | 0.80–0.81 | ≈0.55 |
| $\le 0.75$ [\texttt{f-20261013-005923-526c}] | 0.463–0.487 | 0.746–0.753 | 0.51–0.54 |
| $\le 0.8$ [\texttt{f-20261013-000359-37e2}] | 0.372–0.396 | 0.670–0.672 | 0.455–0.475 |
| $\le 0.85$ [\texttt{f-20261012-034018-5526}] | 0.248–0.333 | 0.507–0.594 | 0.34–0.40 |

The decline steepens above $e \le 0.75$ [\texttt{f-20261013-005923-526c}]. Formation stays at steps 50–100 across these ranges [\texttt{f-20261012-055051-b178}, \texttt{f-20261013-000359-37e2}, \texttt{f-20261013-005923-526c}, \texttt{f-20261012-034018-5526}]. Force is weaker at every ceiling, with normalised margins of 0.11–0.22, and it forms late, at steps 250–590 [\texttt{f-20261012-034018-5526}, \texttt{f-20261012-055051-b178}, \texttt{f-20261013-000359-37e2}, \texttt{f-20261013-005923-526c}].

\subsection{Out-of-distribution transfer}

\textbf{Trained on $e \le 0.5$} [\texttt{f-20261011-203723-b7de}]:

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Low mass ratios} ($m_2 \in [0.01, 0.1]$). Error was about 3.6× the in-distribution error, and force $R^2$ held at 0.75–0.77.
\item \textbf{High eccentricity} ($e \in [0.5, 0.8]$). Error was 16–18× the in-distribution error, so the pre-registered 10× transfer criterion failed. The models still beat the baseline, and force $R^2$ fell to 0.40/0.34.
\item \textbf{Three-body sets.} Ratios of 72 and 70 are exploratory only.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Trained on $e \in [0, 0.8]$} [\texttt{f-20261011-211551-d718}]:

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{High-$e$ error.} The gap closed to 1.01–1.05× the in-distribution error.
\item \textbf{Truncated runs.} Both runs were cut short by the budget stop at step 2500 (completion 0.867 and 0.9), so their probes describe the step-2500 weights.
\item \textbf{Probes still differ.} Even with matched prediction error, force $R^2$ on held-out high-$e$ systems was 0.366–0.377, against 0.729–0.802 on low-$e$ systems. These out-of-distribution probes have no random controls.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Symbolic regression has not recovered $r^{-2}$}

The symbolic fits do not recover the inverse-square law:

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Strong probes} (\texttt{accel\_step} $R^2$ 0.831/0.881). The probe direction fits as $a^2/r - c\,a$ with $c \approx 0.55$, not as the true $a^3/r^2$. Force fits as about $0.8/r$ [\texttt{f-20261011-231118-7a37}].
\item \textbf{Weaker probes at $e \le 0.9$} ($R^2$ 0.40–0.50). These also fit as $r^{-1}$ [\texttt{f-20261011-223027-8fb3}].
\item \textbf{Possible fitter artefact.} The fitter has not been run on the true \texttt{accel\_step} target, so we cannot rule out that it is the source of the $r^{-1}$ form.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Null and negative results}

Twelve hypotheses were refuted:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{A wider $m_2$ range would make force fall behind \texttt{accel\_step} on margin.} It did not [\texttt{f-20261011-203628-5cd9}].
\item \textbf{Training on $e \le 0.5$ would transfer to high $e$ within 10×.} The error was 16–18× [\texttt{f-20261011-203723-b7de}].
\item \textbf{Fixing $m_2 = 0.5$ would lift force $R^2$ to 0.85.} It reached 0.750/0.732, in runs cut short by the budget stop [\texttt{f-20261011-223000-b5f9}].
\item \textbf{Wide-$e$ training would give an $r^{-2}$ fit.} The fits were $r^{-1}$ [\texttt{f-20261011-223027-8fb3}].
\item \textbf{\texttt{accel\_step} would clear a 0.15 bar and the early force spike would vanish under best-of-5 controls.} Neither held [\texttt{f-20261012-005950-42a7}].
\item \textbf{Formation would scale in proportion to the learning rate.} The shift was sub-proportional [\texttt{f-20261012-044258-ab88}].
\item \textbf{$N = 64$ formation would fall in a narrow window.} Only 2 of 5 seeds did [\texttt{f-20261012-054929-0067}].
\item \textbf{$N = 16$ would raise the normalised \texttt{accel\_step} margin.} It was level with $N = 64$ [\texttt{f-20261013-000329-a86b}].
\item \textbf{Force would decline gradually from $N = 16$.} $N = 64$ matched $N = 16$ [\texttt{f-20261013-015914-3f49}].
\item \textbf{An exact $N = 256$ rerun would reproduce the original margins.} It did not [\texttt{f-20261013-050513-2c7a}].
\item \textbf{The $N = 128 \to 256$ force drop comes from the controls alone.} On data seed 0, trained $R^2$ also fell [\texttt{f-20261013-075749-3d2c}].
\item \textbf{1000 extra steps at $N = 256$ would close the gap to $N = 128$.} They did not [\texttt{f-20261013-083108-2efd}].
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Not yet tested, and limits}

The lab has not yet reported:

\begin{itemize}
\item \texttt{velocity} probe results;
\item activation patching, or any other causal test of whether the decoded codes are used;
\item a force probe with \texttt{distance} and \texttt{accel\_step} regressed out;
\item a random-control pool shared across $N$;
\item nonlinear or log-target probes;
\item symbolic regression on the true \texttt{accel\_step} target;
\item a matched-loss $N = 128$ vs $N = 256$ comparison on data seed 1;
\item an explanation of the original $N = 256$ discrepancy.
\end{itemize}

Several limits apply throughout:

\begin{itemize}
\item All probes are linear.
\item \texttt{formed\_at\_step} sometimes lags the trajectory by one checkpoint [\texttt{f-20261012-054929-0067}, \texttt{f-20261013-054512-1b08}].
\item Several paired comparisons rely on 3-decimal trajectory values, whose $R^2$ field may be a maximum over layers [\texttt{f-20261013-075749-3d2c}, \texttt{f-20261013-083108-2efd}].
\item Most results come from one architecture.
\item Six findings use data seed 1 [\texttt{f-20261013-020028-8792}, \texttt{f-20261013-025812-b9ee}, \texttt{f-20261013-054512-1b08}, \texttt{f-20261013-054542-23e1}, \texttt{f-20261013-063631-c4ed}, \texttt{f-20261013-224908-8b46}]. All others use data seed 0.
\end{itemize}
